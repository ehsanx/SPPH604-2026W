Week 7 — Missing data: the ideas on a made-up town, then multiple imputation step by step in MASLD

Duration: ~2.5 h (Tue 20 Oct, before the Thu 22 Oct lab). Deck: lectures/slides/Week7_missing_slides.qmd. Its MASLD numbers come from lectures/analysis/Week7_missing_data.R (written to Week7_missing_data.json). A checker confirms that the deck and this plan quote those outputs verbatim.

The design. Three parts, the same shape as Weeks 5 and 6.

  • Part 1, the ideas on a made-up town of 1,000 adults whose smoking status is hidden three ways (MCAR, MAR on age, MNAR on smoking). The truth is known, so complete cases can be checked: a share and a risk ratio come out differently. Then single fill-ins on real MASLD data, how mice() works, and Rubin’s rules.
  • Part 2, multiple imputation step by step on MASLD, with the R code students reuse in P5: look (Step 0), set up (Step 1), impute and check (Step 2), analyse (Step 3), pool (Step 4), and add Week 6’s survey design (Step 5, in words; its code is Thursday’s lab).
  • Part 3, special cases, limits and reporting: missing outcomes (MID), MNAR sensitivity analysis, what no method fixes, and a methods paragraph students can adapt.

  • 6,048 — the locked domain (MASLD, a positive fasting weight, linked to the death index); everyone imputation uses.
  • 5,963 — complete on L: what coxph() keeps by default (Slide 2), and today’s complete cases.
  • 5,911 — Weeks 4–6’s rows: complete on L and on the paper’s Model 2 covariates (cancer history). Its HRs equal the 5,963’s to two decimals, so the deck compares imputation with the 5,963.
  • 6,018 — the harmonised-smoking sensitivity (A1).
  • The funnel (Slide 26) runs from all 59,842 NHANES 2007–2018 participants to the 5,963 complete on L.
  • The design frame is the whole fasting subsample (17,208 people aged 12 and over, 2,246 of them aged 12–17). It is not analysed; the design is declared on it and the cohort reached with subset() (Step 5).
  • The town (Slides 7–10, 22–23, A5) is made up: 1,000 adults. Slides 18 and 21 use other made-up numbers (one person’s smoking and sedentary time; three log HRs).
  • The fill-in and Little’s-test demonstrations (Slides 12–13, 16) use the 6,048 adults’ BMI and waist, both complete, with waist values hidden on purpose.

What this week does NOT do.

  • It does not re-run the paper’s Model 1. P5’s worked example uses Model 1, labelled as the paper’s specification (Slide 57).

  • It does not impute outcomes (the 2 adults not linked to the death index are outcome ascertainment). MID is explained for Thursday’s lab (Slide 49).

Learning objectives

By the end, students can follow the deck’s workflow (Slide 4) on their own paper:

  1. Look (Step 0). Count the missing values, type every loss (P5’s funnel), compare complete and incomplete rows, and say why each value is missing: never asked, asked differently, not answered, or never sampled.
  2. Judge the mechanism. Explain MCAR, MAR and MNAR in plain words; say what data can and cannot show about them (Little’s test); and say why the same missingness can bias a share but not a risk ratio.
  3. Set up (Step 1). Build an imputation model at least as rich as the analysis model: the outcome (for a Cox model, dead and the Nelson–Aalen H), every analysis variable including interactions, auxiliaries, design information; never the ID.
  4. Impute and check (Step 2). Tell m from maxit; read a trace plot and an observed-vs-imputed plot; say why one completed copy is not enough.
  5. Analyse and pool (Steps 3–4). Fit the model in every copy with with() and pool with Rubin’s rules (pool()), reading estimate, ubar, b and t.
  6. Add the design (Step 5). Say why the design is declared on the whole fasting frame in every completed copy before the MASLD domain is analysed and the results combined.
  7. Report complete cases and imputation side by side, with the assumption, the model, a sensitivity analysis and the population each describes.

They can also say why single imputation (mean, regression) is not enough, and why the outcome belongs in the imputation model.

Part 1 · The ideas, in a made-up town · 53 minutes

Slide 5 — Why missing data matter (2 min)

Bias, wrong precision, lost power. How much is missing mainly affects precision; why it is missing decides bias. A small amount can still bias an estimate.

Slide 6 — Three reasons a value can be missing (3 min)

  • MCAR, MAR, MNAR (Rubin 1976), each in plain words with a smoking example.
  • The grey box: “missing at random” means random once we account for what was observed. Under MAR the observed data contain what is needed to correct the problem; under MNAR they do not.

Slide 7 — A town of 1,000, and its smokers (3 min)

  • Young 600: 120 smokers, of whom 12 die (10%); 480 non-smokers, of whom 24 die (5%).
  • Old 400: 200 smokers, of whom 80 die (40%); 200 non-smokers, of whom 40 die (20%).
  • The truth: 32% smoke, and the RR is 2 in each age group. Age is a confounder: the old smoke more and die more. A new town, not Week 6’s.
  • Ask which question the class expects to be easier from incomplete data, the share or the RR. Most will say the share.

Slide 8 — Ways smoking goes missing (3 min)

  • The figure is w7_mechanisms1.png: three panels, the same population and the same arrows among age, smoking and death; only the arrow into “smoking hidden?” changes. MCAR: none (chance). MAR: from age. MNAR: from smoking itself.
  • For each scenario: can complete cases estimate the share who smoke (p) and the risk ratio (RR)? Slide 9 answers p for all three; Slide 10 answers the RR for MNAR only. If asked about the others: under MCAR and MAR on age the complete-case RR stays 2.00 in each age group, because neither depends on death given age and smoking.

Slide 9 — Complete cases: estimating smoking prevalence (4 min)

  • The table: smokers remaining, people remaining, estimated prevalence. Truth 320 / 1,000 = 32%; MCAR (half of everyone) 160 / 500 = 32%, right; MAR (half of older adults) 220 / 800 = 27.5%, wrong; MNAR (half of smokers) 160 / 840 = 19%, wrong.
  • Why MAR is wrong here: older adults smoke more (50% vs 20%). All 600 young remain with their 120 smokers; half the 400 old remain with 100 smokers: (120 + 100) / (600 + 200) = 220 / 800 = 27.5%.
  • The box: the amount missing does not decide bias. MCAR hid 500 people and was right; MNAR hid 160 and was wrong.

Slide 10 — Same missing data, different conclusions (4 min)

  • MNAR, half the smokers hidden, regardless of death. The share: 320 / 1,000 = 32% becomes 160 / 840 = 19%, biased. The RR among the old: (80 / 200) / (40 / 200) = 2.0 stays (40 / 100) / (40 / 200) = 2.0, unbiased.
  • Why: equal shares of the smokers who died and who survived were hidden, so the risk among the remaining smokers is unchanged.
  • The rule behind it (for the instructor): for an adjusted RR or HR, complete cases are unbiased when missingness does not depend on the outcome given every variable in the model, including the incomplete one — here even under MNAR (White & Carlin 2010; Hughes et al. 2019). A sufficient condition, if the model is right. Bias depends on what is estimated.

Slide 11 — Can the data tell us which mechanism? (2 min)

  • Against MCAR: sometimes (compare complete and incomplete rows; Little’s test, next slide). For MCAR: a non-significant test is not proof. MAR vs MNAR: no test exists.
  • Argue the mechanism from how the data were collected: who was asked, who answered, who was never asked, and why.

Slide 12 — In R: Little’s test on MCAR and MAR data (3 min)

naniar::mcar_test() on the MASLD BMI/waist data, twice. Waist hidden for 1,814 adults at random: statistic 0.175 on 1 df, p = 0.676. Waist hidden for about 60% of adults with BMI above 35: statistic 1838.456, p < 0.001. The slide states the null hypothesis and how to read p.

Slide 13 — In R: Little’s test on MCAR and MAR data (2 min)

The two results as a table: p = 0.676, no evidence against MCAR (not proof of it); p < 0.001, evidence against MCAR. The box: the test cannot prove MCAR, cannot tell MAR from MNAR, and cannot say whether complete cases are biased. The notes read missing.patterns = 2.

Slide 14 — Quick fixes that cause trouble (2 min)

Complete cases without thinking; the mean; a “missing” category or indicator (Vach & Blettner 1991; Greenland & Finkle 1995); last value carried forward. Any single filled-in value treated as measured makes standard errors too small.

Slide 15 — Single imputation: four ways to fill a gap (2 min)

The method table: mean ("mean"), regression prediction ("norm.predict"), stochastic regression ("norm.nob"), predictive mean matching ("pmm"); what fills each gap, and what goes wrong when it is done once. Box: any of these done once is single imputation; MI repeats proper draws (which also redraw the model’s coefficients) and pools.

Slide 16 — Single imputation, in a picture (3 min)

  • The figure is w7_single_imp.png: the MASLD adults’ BMI and waist, with 1,814 waist values hidden at random and filled in once three ways.
  • SD of waist: truth 13.0 cm; mean fill 10.9; regression 12.4; stochastic 13.0.
  • Correlation with BMI: truth 0.83; mean 0.70; regression 0.87; stochastic 0.83.
  • The box reads one panel at a time: the mean shrinks the spread and weakens the correlation; regression exaggerates the correlation; noise keeps both, but one copy still hides the uncertainty.

Slide 17 — Multiple imputation in three steps (3 min)

  • The figure is w7_mi_workflow.png: incomplete data → mice() → m copies (mids) → with() → m results (mira) → pool() → one result (mipo). Last year’s Lumley picture.
  • The table: each verb, its R line, the object R prints and what it holds; complete(imp, 1) returns copy 1 as an ordinary data frame.

Slide 18 — How MICE works: one person, one iteration (3 min)

Made-up numbers: one person missing both smoking and sedentary time. Start 0 / 300 min; update smoking (predicted probability 20%, the random draw says smoker) → 1 / 300; update sedentary time from the new smoking (PMM borrows an observed 410 minutes) → 1 / 410; repeat with the latest values. Each filled-in value helps predict the other.

Slide 19 — How MICE works: iterations (maxit = 10) vs. imputations (m = 20) (3 min)

Within each copy, every incomplete variable is updated 10 times (maxit); only the last round is kept. Twenty separate chains give 20 completed copies (m). Iterations let a chain stabilise (the trace plot checks it, Slide 37); copies carry the uncertainty about the missing values. More iterations do not replace more copies.

Slide 20 — How mice() fills several variables (2 min)

The figure is w7_chained.png: random starting values; smoking from everything else; sedentary time from everything else including the new smoking; repeat; one copy; all of it m times. Observed values never change. Slide 18’s one person was one pass of it (the box says so).

Slide 21 — Within and between: three copies, by hand (3 min)

Made-up numbers: log HRs 0.40, 0.70, 1.00, each with SE 0.20. Pooled 0.70; within Ū = 0.04; between B = 0.09; T = 0.04 + (1 + 1/3) × 0.09 = 0.16; SE = 0.40, double each copy’s. Pool variances, not SEs, on the log scale.

Slide 22 — Rubin’s rules: combining the copies (3 min)

  • The figure is w7_rubin_town.png: the town’s copies disagree, so the pooled interval is wider than any single copy’s. The width of each grey line is within; how far the dots wander is between. The MASLD panel comes at Slide 43.
  • The formula: pooled estimate = the average; total variance = Ū + (1 + 1/m) B.
  • Grey box, in numbers (m = 100, log RR among the old): Ū = 0.03802, B = 0.02400, T = 0.03802 + 1.01 × 0.02400 = 0.06226, so the SE grows from 0.195 to 0.250. The full calculation in R is A5.
  • Say this. The figure and the box use a fourth version of the town that the deck no longer shows: smoking hidden for half of those who died. Death is recorded for everyone, so that is MAR, and imputing smoking from age and death recovers the RR. One sentence is enough.

Slide 23 — What the town taught us (3 min)

Three lessons: bias depends on what is estimated (the share was wrong, the RR right); complete cases are not always biased, even under MNAR; MI is not automatically better (under MAR it can reduce bias if the model includes the outcome; under MNAR it can still be biased). Box: ask what you are estimating, why values are missing, and what your method assumes. The notes call the break.

Slide 31 — Step 0: Who has missing data? (3 min)

  • On L: complete 5,963, incomplete 85. Mean age 51.1 vs 32.0; died 9.0% vs 3.5%. Missingness is related to what we can see, so MCAR is unlikely.
  • The box, the Cox rule: a difference is a reason to investigate, not proof of bias. For an adjusted Cox model the question is whether being a complete case depends on the event process given the model’s covariates.
  • Notes: a share dying is not a hazard; only three deaths among the 85, so mortality comparisons are imprecise.

Slide 32 — Step 1: What goes into the imputation model? (3 min)

  • The analysis variables (the obesity–waist groups, age, sex, race, smoking, sedentary time), the outcome (dead and the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard H; White & Royston 2009), auxiliaries (survey cycle, age groups) and design information (the log weight); never the ID.
  • The line: dat$H <- nelsonaalen(dat, time_yr, dead). Raw time stays for Surv() but is not a predictor.
  • For the TA: strata as a factor, and PSU indicators within strata (never the raw PSU number), are fuller design options (Reiter et al. 2006). The choices are not equally complete: say which and why.

Slide 35 — Step 2: how many copies and iterations? (2 min)

  • Rules for m: 3–5 (Rubin 1987); at least the % of incomplete rows (White, Royston & Wood 2011); 20–100 (Austin et al. 2021).

  • More copies reduce simulation noise; they do not fix the model or MNAR.

Slide 38 — Step 2, check 1: How to read the trace plots (2 min)

Good: lines fluctuate around a stable level, overlap, no trend. Concerning: drift, or chains that stay apart. The trace plot judges maxit, not m; convergence does not show the imputed values are right.

Slide 39 — Step 2, check 2: observed vs filled in (2 min)

Part 3 · Special cases, limits and reporting · 16 minutes

Slide 49 — When the outcome is missing: MID (2 min)

Impute everything including the outcome; delete the rows whose outcome was imputed; analyse and pool (von Hippel 2007). Useful when many outcomes are missing. Not a default: MID can be biased when an auxiliary variable predicts both the outcome and whether it is missing; then keep the imputed outcomes (Sullivan et al. 2015). L6’s question 3 uses MID; its code issue is TA note 5, not on the slide. Our outcome is complete: the 2 adults not linked are outcome ascertainment.

Slide 50 — When MNAR is plausible: a sensitivity analysis (2 min)

Delta adjustment (shift the filled-in values by a delta, e.g. 30 minutes = 0.15 on the standardised scale; for smoking, add the delta to the log-odds in its imputation model before the values are drawn; refit and pool at each delta). One value, worked: a filled-in 300 minutes becomes 330 under delta = +30; an observed 250 stays 250; repeat for delta = −30, 0, +30, +60 (not run today). In mice: post for sedentary time, method "mnar.logreg" for smoking. Slide 47’s extreme fills are selected scenarios, not bounds; more imputations do not fix MNAR.

Slide 51 — What missing-data methods cannot fix (2 min)

Never sampled (the weight; its nonresponse adjustment also covers the 323 with a fasting weight of 0), never asked, asked differently, MNAR, selection on FLI ≥ 60, unmeasured confounding.

  1. What it is.
    • Data: Williams et al. 2021 (Prev Med), NHANES 2015–2018. 4,746 adults with diabetes or hypertension; 4,716 have complete outcomes and exposures.
    • Model: survey-weighted logistic regression (svyglm), not a Cox model.

  3. m = 5 is a time-saving shortcut. The “m ≥ % incomplete” rule gives 21 for these data.
  4. Design variables in the imputation model. The exercise text says to use strata but not the weight, PSU or ID; its code drops only DIQ010 and BPQ020, so id, survey.weight, psu and strata all become predictors.

    • The weight: keep it, as Tuesday did (log weight), or zero it, as the lab’s text says. The choices are not equally complete (Reiter et al. 2006); say which and why. Strata: a factor.
    • The fix barely changes the result (advice OR 1.846 → 1.845).
  5. Question 3 (“MID”) imputes no outcomes or exposures. It reuses question 2’s meth, which gives those variables method "". Fix: build make.method() on the question 3 data; record a missing-outcome indicator before mice().

Known differences from P5’s materials

  • Specification and rows. The P5 handout and worked example use the paper’s Model 1 on 5,939 rows. This lecture uses L on 5,963 and 6,048 rows (and recalls Weeks 4–6’s 5,911).
  • The 109 missing cancer values. P5 treats them as ordinary missingness, “MAR given the Model-1 covariates and the outcome”, and runs an “all have cancer” extreme. They are a questionnaire skip: every one is an 18–19-year-old, and the question starts at age 20.
  • “Exactly one Model-1 covariate carries any missingness.” The P5 handout and worked example say this, and the M1 exemplar repeats it through P5.json. The data say otherwise: mi has 109 never-asked values coded “no”; htn has 96 people without a BP reading coded “not hypertensive”.
  • P5’s population sentence should say that its complete-case estimate covers adults aged 20+.
  • Strata in P5’s imputation model. SDMVSTRA enters as a number. Strata are labels: use a factor or leave them out.
  • P5’s worked-example funnel orders the steps differently (fasting frame before adults), uses “DISEASE”, and calls 6,052 → 6,048 “disease + small missingness”. The handout and this deck (Slide 26) type the 2 unlinked as outcome ascertainment.
